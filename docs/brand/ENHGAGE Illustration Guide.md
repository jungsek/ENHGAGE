# ENHGAGE Illustration Guide

---

## Introduction

You know an ENHGAGE illustration when you see one. That is because we have developed a consistent visual language that is uniquely ours. This guide provides a framework for creating artwork that feels right at home in the expanding ENHGAGE universe.

---

## Shape Language

### Construction

All of our illustrations are made from three basic shapes: the **rounded rectangle**, the **circle**, and the **rounded triangle**. You will probably find yourself using the rounded rectangle the most. It is okay to use your pathfinder tool to cut shapes up as you need them. Just make sure that every shape has rounded edges. Pointy shapes are off-brand.

### Rhythm

It is the rhythm of simple shapes that makes our illustrations interesting. Imagine each illustration is a melody. No one wants to hear the same note played over and over, but if you switch between longer and shorter notes, the ear gets interested. Shapes with similar visual weight are predictable and uninteresting, while a variation in shapes gives the eye something exciting to look at. We want to create a character that, beyond being visually pleasing, actually communicates a story through their design. Rhythm helps with that.

### Simplicity

An illustration with good rhythm can still be simple. To use the music analogy again: Simple songs can be powerful, but there is more pressure on each note to strengthen the song. The trick is to make each shape matter in the illustration. Stylistically and practically, we need to use the fewest shapes possible.

| Shape Count | Assessment |
|-------------|------------|
| ~6 shapes | Not enough shapes, too abstract |
| ~15 shapes | Good! |
| ~30 shapes | Too many shapes |

### Objects in Space

ENHGAGE characters and icons are designed on a flat perspective. Depth can still be conveyed as long as it is on the same line of sight.

**Shadows and Shading:** Shadows always appear below characters and objects as a pill shape—never an oval, because ovals imply perspective. The shadow's color depends on the background. Never put a shadow under an illustration that is not a darker color than the base it is sitting on.

## Color

Our illustrations use a wide variety of colors. It is understandable that some illustrations will need a color outside of the palette, but use these colors as a general guide and be consistent where you can.

### Illustration Color Palette

The following colors are approved for use in ENHGAGE illustrations:

| Category | Colors |
|----------|--------|
| **Neutrals** | Cloud (#F7F7F7), Mist (#E5E5E5), Silver (#AFAFAF), Slate (#777777), Charcoal (#4B4B4B) |
| **Teals** | Seafoam (#B2D8D8), Light Teal (#66B2B2), Deep Teal (#008080), Dark Teal (#006666), Midnight Teal (#004C4C) |
| **Warm Accents** | Peach (#FFDAB9), Coral (#FF7F50), Sunset (#FF6347), Amber (#FFBF00), Gold (#FFD700) |
| **Cool Accents** | Sky (#87CEEB), Periwinkle (#CCCCFF), Lavender (#E6E6FA), Slate Blue (#6A5ACD), Mint (#98FF98) |

### Assigning Color

Be as playful as you would like. We want our illustrations to be colorful and vibrant. However, keep the following guidelines in mind:

**Pastels vs. Gray:** All of our apps and services are designed with white backgrounds. This prevents us from using white as a base color. Instead, use the light pastels in our palette. But never use gray—it appears lifeless and cold compared to the rest of our bright palette.

**Too Much Color:** Keep your illustration to a few colors. Too many colors can hurt legibility when an object scales to a small size.

### Floating Accents

We will occasionally have certain objects float above or below a character or object. Floating objects give us flexibility when turning an object in space, and they help make posing easier. Only float an object if it serves the illustration—do not force it.

## Characters

ENHGAGE characters are rather simple to construct, but our characters need to scale in terms of both size and production. When the pieces are simple, a character is much easier to pose in other positions. But there is still room for uniqueness.

### Body Types

Our characters are diverse, quirky, and lovable. All of the pieces—head width and height, head shape, facial features, and so on—are customizable. Usually, the head and body are composed of 1–2 basic shapes each. One way of keeping a character's shape language cohesive is to use shape repetition throughout their design. While this is not always required, it is a good starting point.

### Eyes

We use five main eye styles in our characters: **round**, **glasses**, **almond**, **linear**, and **dots** (dots only recommended if a character always appears at a small scale). Different eye styles can be explored, but make sure they are geometric in nature. No ovals!

**Emotions:** Eyes are an incredible tool for conveying our characters' personalities and state of mind. You could enlarge or shrink the pupil, suggest tears with a little shiny-eye effect, or lower the eyelids. All these effects make our characters feel both playful and relatable.

**Eye Don'ts:**
- Do not use small glasses, because they hide eye emotions.
- Do not center pupils vertically within the eye.
- Do not use shapes other than a fully-rounded "pill" for eyes.

### Noses

Noses are made up of 1–2 rounded rectangles. The size of the nose is up to you and the character you are creating. When coloring the nose, make sure it is as saturated as it is dark. When in profile, the nose should take on the color of the base skin tone.

### Mouths

Mouths are expressive and the least geometric shape in our style. Most mouths favor one side. This asymmetry is more lifelike and brings more emotion to the face. The mouth can break out of the frame of the face to convey more extreme emotions. The teeth and tongue should always be centered inside the mouth.

### Hair

Hair should be simple but interesting. Too many irregular shapes will make the hair unnecessarily complex and difficult to turn in space. If possible, compose hair with just one or two large shapes.

### Arms and Hands

These can be the most time-consuming parts for illustrators and animators when posing and animating. Try to keep hands as abstract as you can by keeping them as circles in most places. If you need to show fingers, display the bare minimum needed for the pose. Never use more than 4 fingers.

### Posing

Posing a character adds personality and quirk to their design and ultimately tells more of their story. As much as possible, try not to use characters in a static, expressionless state, which could make them feel lifeless. Not all poses will work for certain characters. You will need to explore what works for their character within the ENHGAGE style.

## Mascot Guidelines

If ENHGAGE has a mascot character, the following guidelines apply:

### Perspective

The mascot's most iconic features should be carefully managed when turning in space. Dragging anchor points from side-to-side will give the illusion of a head turn. But do not slide it too far—it creates an unnatural pinch.

### Joints

The mascot can bend at the waist, elbow, and wrists. When bending at the waist, use a rounded rectangle to place over the body instead of editing the existing shape.

### Mascot Don'ts

- Do not center the mascot's pupils within its eyes—it makes it look creepy.
- Do not give the mascot too many fingers.
- Do not make the mascot's limbs a slightly darker color when they are posed over or behind its body. When posed correctly, an additional color is not necessary.
